# The nutrition basis for an Open Food Facts product is inferred, per provider, never declared

A product's nutrition basis — what the per-100 values denominate — has to be known before those values can be scaled by a consumed amount. Open Food Facts does not supply it reliably, so the decision is that an OFF product carries an **inferred** basis derived from its portion dimension (MASS gives per 100 g, VOLUME gives per 100 ml, UNKNOWN gives none), recorded as inferred rather than declared, and scoped explicitly to Open Food Facts rather than adopted as a domain-wide rule.

The obvious candidate for a declared basis is OFF's own `nutrition_data_per`, and it was rejected on evidence gathered against the live API (`docs/off-nutrition-basis-evidence.md`). That field is documented with only the values `100g` and `serving`; across products fetched from the single-product endpoint this app actually calls it was absent or the literal string `"100g"`, including for a 1 L Coca-Cola and a 1.25 L water. Worse, grouping products by it produces no separation in their nutrient values: olive oils declaring `100g` have a median 824 kcal per 100 against 828 for those declaring nothing, where the physically correct figures are ~884 per 100 g and ~810 per 100 ml. Contributors evidently transcribe the printed figure whichever option they select. Treating the field as a declared basis would therefore have made roughly one in five volume products unloggable as a false contradiction, on the strength of a flag that does not track the numbers.

The inference rests on OFF's arithmetic convention instead. OFF derives its per-serving nutriments as `per-100 value * serving_quantity / 100` regardless of whether the serving unit is grams or millilitres — 186 of 186 sampled products agreed within 2%. That demonstrates OFF treats its per-100 fields as "per 100 of the product's own quantity unit". It is provider-semantic evidence, not proof about the physical label, and the check is circular as proof of the latter since the per-serving values are themselves derived; the inference is accepted as a documented provider convention, not as a fact about the world.

Keeping inferred distinct from declared, rather than collapsing both into one `nutritionBasis`, is the part most likely to look like unnecessary ceremony later. It is what lets a genuine declared basis — from a person reading a physical label, an admin, or a future provider with a real basis field — contradict the portion dimension and correctly mark a product as not scalable, while the same apparent contradiction from OFF is never manufactured. It also means the inference rule cannot silently spread: a future provider inherits no basis inference until its own semantics are investigated and written down. The cost is a provenance concept the calculator never reads, since the calculator only ever consumes the effective basis.

The alternative was strictness: require a declared basis, and accept that no OFF product can be logged. That is coherent and was seriously considered, because it is the only option with no inference at all. It was rejected because it removes barcode logging entirely rather than making its unit handling correct, and because an inference that is labelled, scoped to one provider, and backed by written evidence is a different thing from the silent grams assumption this work exists to delete.
